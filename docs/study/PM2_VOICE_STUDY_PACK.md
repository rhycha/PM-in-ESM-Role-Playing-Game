# PM² voice study pack for CASTOR and POLLUX

Use this attachment to study project management through two connected fictional projects. It contains the background the learner needs, a sequence of short conversations, and answer guidance for the tutor. Begin with understanding the situation; introduce the formal PM² term after its purpose is clear.

**CASTOR replaces a lending operations system. POLLUX uses its data to build a reporting portal.** The learner plays the Project Manager in both. The central question is usually: what decision is needed, who can make it, and what evidence supports it?

The European Stability Mechanism is a real institution. CASTOR, POLLUX, Levallois Systems, their budgets, events and people are fictional teaching cases. Historical names are mnemonic personas, not real employees or historical portrayals. Institutional figures and the treaty situation in the original case are background assumptions, not a statement of current conditions. This pack is study material, not official examination material or a claim of certification coverage.

## Instructions for the voice tutor

**Use a short conversation, not a spoken lecture.** Start by locating the learner: ask one question about what they think the project is trying to improve. If they cannot answer, give the orientation below first. Do not test unexplained jargon.

Use this rhythm for each turn:

1. Address the learner's actual answer or question. Correct a specific misunderstanding directly, without flattery.
2. Give one small scaffold: a plain definition, a concrete comparison, or one step of a worked example.
3. Ask one focused question and stop. Do not put three questions inside one sentence.

Usually speak for twenty to forty seconds before returning the floor. The learner can request a longer explanation. Give them time to think; silence is not evidence of failure. If the voice transcript is ambiguous, confirm the disputed number or term before correcting the learner.

**When the learner is stuck:** narrow the question once; then explain the missing concept and work an example. Ask them to apply it to a slightly different situation. If they request a direct answer, provide it before offering practice. Socratic teaching must not become answer withholding.

Before each scenario, name the project, phase, speaker's role, immediate problem and decision. For example: “We are in CASTOR, during execution. You are the Project Manager. A senior stakeholder wants two extra screens. The team has not assessed the cost. We need to decide the next management action.” Then ask only the session's first question.

Use role names before character names until the learner knows the cast. Say “the Project Owner, Florence” rather than assuming “Florence” explains her authority. In POLLUX, always distinguish **Project Owner** from **Product Owner** aloud. Do not abbreviate both to “P O”.

For numbers, say what is measured before the number. Say “the budget is eight point four million euros”. Read identifiers as labels: “risk R zero seven”, “issue I fourteen”, “change request zero one four”. Read “RfP” as “Ready for Planning”, not “request for proposal”. Explain one calculation at a time; distinguish exact figures from rounded figures. Ask for an interpretation before demanding mental arithmetic.

Treat all exercises below as private self-study. Use the answer guidance to diagnose reasoning, not to recite the answer before the learner tries. Accept equivalent explanations in ordinary language. Do not claim a topic is mastered because one answer is correct. After success, try one transfer question; after that, move on.

Do not invent guide page numbers, official exam rules, institutional facts, story events or results. A new example is allowed if explicitly labelled “a new hypothetical example”. When the pack and an attached official guide disagree, identify the conflict and use the guide for methodology. For scene-specific fictional facts, use the case sources and the source caveats below.

End a session when the learner requests it or after roughly fifteen to twenty minutes. Give a short recap and the progress handoff. Do not promise to remember progress across future chats; the learner can paste the handoff into the next one.

## Learner orientation

### The business before the project

In this fictional setting, a lending institution must administer loans and move approved money correctly. A loan agreement sets the terms. Operations turns those terms into payment instructions. Treasury and the payment systems process the movement of money. Staff then compare records to make sure the systems agree.

That comparison is **reconciliation**. If one system says a loan payment is one amount and another says something different, staff must find the reason. The **value date** is the financial date on which the payment takes effect; the case gives payment processing a daily cut-off. A wrong reference or late instruction can matter even when the screen looks correct.

An **audit trail** records who did what and when. **Segregation of duties** separates incompatible responsibilities, so one person cannot prepare and approve everything without an independent check. These controls matter because a fast system that moves money incorrectly is not a successful solution.

The project budget pays for building and introducing the system. It is not the amount of money lent to countries. A billion-euro loan and an eight-million-euro software project are different quantities.

### What CASTOR changes

Before CASTOR, staff retype payment information across several systems. The old Lending Operations System, abbreviated LOS, is difficult to change. Reconciliation takes too long, and Internal Audit has two unresolved findings.

CASTOR builds a configurable lending platform and connects the work from loan agreement through payment and repayment schedules. It also changes how people work: staff need training, the new system needs a safe transition into use, and someone must operate it after the project team leaves.

| Measure | Starting position in the case | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Disbursement cycle | Five working days | One working day |
| Reconciliation | Three working days | Same day |
| Adding a new lending instrument | Nine months | Six weeks |
| Payment fields retyped manually | About 1,400 each quarter | Zero |
| Open LOS audit findings | Two | Zero |

The approved case budget is **€8.4 million**, with a stated effort of **3,100 person-days**. One person-day is one person's work for one working day. It is effort, not the number of calendar days before delivery. The case mixes internal staff and an external supplier, Levallois Systems. It reports a final cost of **€8.96 million**.

### What POLLUX changes

CASTOR leaves the organisation with usable lending data. It does not solve every reporting problem. Different audiences still request different reports, and staff assemble them using eleven spreadsheets. Four analysts spend five working days preparing each quarter-end reporting pack.

POLLUX builds the **Member-State Exposure Reporting Portal**. “Exposure” here means the institution's lending position towards a country or other category; “maturity” concerns when amounts fall due. The portal shows authorised users information by country, lending instrument and maturity, with a trace back to the lending platform.

Risk wants stress scenarios. The Board wants a short overview. Finance wants figures that reconcile to the accounts. Users learn what they need by seeing working reports. This uncertainty makes iterative delivery useful: build a small usable part, show it, learn and revise priorities.

The case budget is **€4.6 million**, with **1,850 person-days** of planned effort. Iterations last **two weeks**. There are four planned releases in 2029. The initiation request is dated **04 Sep 2028**, the first iteration **08 Jan 2029**, and administrative closure **20 Mar 2030**.

| Measure | Starting position in the case | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Quarter-end pack preparation | Five days with four analysts | One day with one analyst |
| New report view | About one quarter | One two-week iteration |
| Manually maintained spreadsheets | Eleven | Zero |
| Figures traceable without manual mapping | About forty per cent | One hundred per cent |
| Reporting version-control audit findings | Two | Zero |

PM²-Agile still has a Business Case, project governance, phases, gates and a Project Manager. It adds iterative development practices. “Agile” does not mean no plan, unlimited changes or automatic success.

## Who is speaking and what they care about

Learn the first five roles before the rest. The short descriptions explain the case; they do not replace an activity-specific responsibility matrix.

| Role | Fictional persona | What their perspective adds |
|---|---|---|
| Project Manager or PM | You | Coordinate delivery, maintain the management records, expose problems and obtain decisions within the agreed authority. |
| Project Owner or PO | Florence Nightingale | Own the business outcome and benefits; decide whether the project continues to serve the business need. |
| Business Manager or BM | Grace Hopper | Represent business needs in daily work and coordinate adoption by business users. |
| Solution Provider or SP | Leonardo da Vinci | Take accountability for the solution and the resources needed to deliver it. |
| Project Steering Committee or PSC | Committee chaired by Florence | Resolve significant project matters under the project's governance arrangements. Florence acting as chair is not identical to Florence acting alone as Project Owner. |
| Appropriate Governance Body or AGB | Corporate Investment Board chaired by Lorenzo de' Medici | Make investment and portfolio decisions beyond the project's own management authority. |
| User Representative | Marie Curie in CASTOR | Test whether the deliverable supports the real job; provide validation evidence. The Project Owner gives formal deliverable acceptance in this case. |
| Project Core Team or PCT | Technical and project specialists | Perform the project work. Nikola Tesla is the lead architect whose departure becomes a management problem. |
| Project Quality Assurance or PQA | W. Edwards Deming | Review the project's management process independently of the Project Manager. |
| Project Support Office or PSO | Henry Gantt | Help with schedules, records, versions and archiving. |

POLLUX adds the following development roles:

| Role | Fictional persona | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Product Owner or PrOw | Marie Curie | Prioritise the Work Items List, clarify product value and acceptance criteria, and make product decisions with business input. |
| Team Coordinator or TeCo | Ada Lovelace | Facilitate the team's collaboration and ceremonies; help make impediments and progress visible. |
| Architecture Owner or ArOw | Albert Einstein | Guide technical coherence and explain why enabling technical work is needed. |
| Agile Team Member or ATeM | Hedy Lamarr, Charles Babbage, Gustave Eiffel | Build and test the working product with the team. |

**Project Owner and Product Owner are separate roles.** Florence remains concerned with the project's business case and benefits. Marie decides product priorities in POLLUX, working with Grace and other stakeholders. The Project Manager coordinates the project; being responsible for a process does not grant every approval power inside it.

In RASCI, **Responsible** does the work, **Accountable** owns the activity's result or approval, **Supporting** helps perform it, **Consulted** provides input, and **Informed** receives information. Read the row for the specific activity. Do not infer all approvals from someone's title. For example, the case distinguishes Steering Committee approval of the Project-End Report from the Project Owner's final approval at administrative closure.

Optional French bridges help with meaning while preserving the English PM² terms:

| English term | French bridge | Distinction to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Deliverable | Livrable | A result the project produces, not automatically a realised benefit. |
| Benefit | Bénéfice or avantage attendu | The useful effect for the organisation; it need not be financial profit. |
| Stakeholder | Partie prenante | Anyone who can affect or be affected by the project. |
| Scope | Périmètre | What the project includes and excludes. |
| Baseline | Référence approuvée | The approved comparison point, not the latest forecast. |
| Forecast | Prévision | A current estimate of what may happen. |
| Reconciliation | Rapprochement des données | Checking that records agree and explaining differences. |
| Issue | Problème avéré nécessitant une action | Here, an event that already needs management action; not the French word “issue” meaning an exit or outcome. |
| Accountable and Responsible | Redevable du résultat and chargé de réaliser | French “responsable” can hide the distinction; preserve the English RASCI letters. |

## The management map

### Phases and gates

**Initiating** establishes the reason for the project and its initial definition. **Planning** agrees how it will be delivered and managed. **Executing** produces and introduces the deliverables. **Closing** records the result, completes formal acceptance and administrative work, and hands remaining responsibilities to named owners.

The three named phase gates are **Ready for Planning**, **Ready for Executing** and **Ready for Closing**. A gate is a decision to progress, supported by evidence. The checklist helps prepare that decision; a percentage on a checklist is not a substitute for judgement or authority. Monitor and Control operates across the lifecycle rather than forming a separate final phase. Administrative closure is not a fourth named phase gate.

### Documents solve different problems

| Document or record | The question it helps answer |
|---|---|
| Project Initiation Request | Is this need worth investigating as a project? |
| Business Case | Why invest, which option is justified, and what benefits are expected? |
| Project Charter | What project are we authorising at a high level? |
| Project Handbook | How will this project be governed and managed? |
| Project Work Plan | What work is planned, when, by whom and with what effort or cost? |
| Risk Log | What uncertain event could affect objectives, and what response is planned? |
| Issue Log | What has happened that needs management action, and who is resolving it? |
| Change Request Form and Change Log | What alteration is requested, what is its impact, and what decision was made? |
| Decision Log | What was decided, by whom and on what basis? |
| Deliverables Acceptance Plan | What evidence and process will make acceptance possible? |
| Business Implementation Plan | How will the business adopt the change and realise benefits? |
| Transition Plan | How will the solution move safely into operational use? |
| Project-End Report | What happened, what was learned, and what must continue after closure? |

A **baseline** is an approved reference against which change and performance are assessed. Updating a forecast tells us what may happen. Changing the baseline requires the agreed approval process. Moving a baseline just to hide a delay destroys the comparison.

In POLLUX, the **Development Work Plan** connects three planning levels: the **Work Items List** contains candidate work; the **Release Plan** groups intended delivery over time; the **Iteration Plan** describes the next short period in more detail.

An item's **acceptance criteria** specify what that item must do. The team's **Definition of Done** states shared completion standards, such as required checks. Passing the first does not automatically satisfy the second. The Definition of Done can be improved through the agreed process; it is not permanently frozen.

An **Iteration Review** examines working results and gets feedback. An **Iteration Retrospective** examines how the team worked and chooses improvements. **Release Planning** revisits priorities and the release outlook. **Iteration Planning** selects and plans feasible near-term work. The **Daily Stand-up** helps the team coordinate its work; turning it into a management status interrogation changes its purpose.

## Numbers explained for listening

These worked examples are reference material. The tutor should select one when needed and speak it step by step, not read every calculation in one turn.

### Effort and elapsed time

Hypothetical example: four people each work for five full working days. Effort is four times five, or **twenty person-days**. Elapsed time is **five working days**. This assumes the four people really spend the full period on that work.

Applied to POLLUX's preparation target, the simple full-time comparison is twenty person-days before and one person-day after: a saving of nineteen person-days per pack, or ninety-five per cent. Elapsed preparation time falls from five days to one day: an eighty per cent reduction. These are different percentages because they measure different things. The person-day calculation is an illustrative assumption, not a measured result in the case.

### Risk scoring

In the case, likelihood and impact are each scored from one to five. Likelihood three times impact four gives a **risk score of twelve**. Say: “Likelihood is three out of five. Impact is four out of five. Multiplying gives twelve on the risk scoring scale.”

This is a prioritisation score. It is not a twelve per cent probability. The risk response and escalation follow the project's agreed scales and thresholds. CASTOR s15 uses colour bands of two or below green, three to sixteen yellow, and twenty or above red. It separately assigns scores of six or below to the Project Manager, eight to fifteen to the Project Owner, and sixteen or above to the Steering Committee. A score of sixteen can therefore be yellow and still require escalation to the Committee. Colour and decision authority are different classifications. These are case conventions, not a universal project rule.

### Earned value without confusing it with revenue

CASTOR scene s33 gives these exact values:

| Quantity | Meaning | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Planned Value or PV | Budgeted value of work planned by the reporting date | €3,150,000 |
| Earned Value or EV | Budgeted value of work actually completed by that date | €2,898,000 |
| Actual Cost or AC | What that completed work actually cost | €3,220,000 |

Earned value is measured using the project budget; it is not sales revenue or the value of loans paid out.

The **Cost Performance Index**, CPI, is earned value divided by actual cost: 2,898,000 divided by 3,220,000 equals **0.90**. Say: “For every euro spent, we have completed ninety cents of budgeted work.”

The **Schedule Performance Index**, SPI, is earned value divided by planned value: 2,898,000 divided by 3,150,000 equals **0.92**. We have earned ninety-two per cent of the budgeted work value planned by this point. This is not a direct statement of days late.

Cost variance is EV minus AC: **minus €322,000**. Schedule variance is EV minus PV: **minus €252,000**, expressed in budget-value units, not days. A negative result here means performance is below the comparison baseline.

If current cost efficiency persists, the simple forecast is €8,400,000 divided by 0.90, or approximately **€9.33 million**. That is a conditional forecast, not an approved new budget. Say: “About nine point three three million euros, if cost efficiency continues at the same rate.”

### Final cost and rounding

CASTOR's reported final cost is €8,960,000 against an €8,400,000 baseline. The difference is **€560,000**. Dividing 560,000 by 8,400,000 gives approximately **6.7 per cent over budget**. The earlier €9.33 million forecast and the later €8.96 million result belong to different dates and are not interchangeable.

### Quality and a high percentage

In CASTOR, 998 out of 1,000 loans reconcile and two do not. That is **99.8 per cent** matching. Say: “Nine hundred and ninety-eight of one thousand loans match. Two still need attention.”

A high percentage cannot decide acceptance on its own. Check the agreed acceptance criterion, the defect's consequences and the authorised decision. The case discusses conditional or partial acceptance with recorded defects and a resolution plan. It does not teach that every two defects are acceptable, or that every imperfect deliverable must be rejected.

### Agile forecasting

POLLUX scene s18 has **202 story points** remaining and **five iterations** available. At a forecast **thirty points per iteration**, the team forecasts 150 points in that period, leaving a gap of **fifty-two points**.

Say: “We expect to complete one hundred and fifty points before the release. We have two hundred and two points left. The gap is fifty-two points.” Story points are relative estimates within this team's work. Do not convert them into hours by a universal formula or treat velocity as a guaranteed target. The management discussion concerns scope, dependencies, capacity, date and uncertainty.

## Twelve short study sessions

Each session has one opening question. Follow-up questions are optional and must be asked in separate turns. Read the matching answer guidance only after attempting the problem, unless you prefer explanation first. Session numbers are local navigation labels, not permanent language-learning codes.

| Session | Context and learning goal | First question |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Understand the work | CASTOR begins because payment operations are slow and fragile. Distinguish the business problem from the proposed software. | What job do the staff need to perform more reliably? |
| 2. Justify the project | Initiating. A senior manager wants procurement to start immediately. Understand why comparing investment options matters. | What needs to be established before choosing a supplier? |
| 3. Locate authority | CASTOR. You coordinate the project, Florence owns the business outcome, Leonardo owns the solution. Separate doing work from authorising decisions. | Does being Project Manager mean you can approve any decision needed to finish? |
| 4. Plan adoption | Planning. The system needs migration and forty loan administrators need training. Distinguish transition from business adoption. | Which plan should explain how those administrators learn the new way of working? |
| 5. Handle a change | Executing. A senior stakeholder asks verbally for extra common-backstop screens in Release 2. | What is your first management action? |
| 6. Manage a risk that happens | CASTOR. Risk R-07 concerns losing the architect; on 04 Oct 2027 he resigns. | What changes in the management records once he has resigned? |
| 7. Interpret performance | CASTOR. CPI is zero point nine zero. Practise meaning before calculation. | What does that tell you about spending and completed work? |
| 8. Accept on evidence | CASTOR. Nine hundred and ninety-eight loans match; two do not. | What must you examine before recommending acceptance? |
| 9. Close without losing benefits | CASTOR closes in July 2028; the full-year benefit statement concerns 2029. | What must be handed over so benefit measurement continues? |
| 10. Explain agile governance | POLLUX. Reporting requirements change as users see working reports. | Why might short iterations help this project? |
| 11. Separate product roles and quality | POLLUX. Marie prioritises work; a report meets its own criteria but lacks a required accessibility check. | Can it count as Done yet? |
| 12. Learn and forecast | POLLUX. The team forecasts 150 points before a release but has 202 left. | What decision does this gap require people to discuss? |

Suggested transfer prompts, used one at a time after an initial answer:

- After Session 3: “Who owns the Work Items List priorities in POLLUX, and how is that different from Florence's role?”
- After Session 5: “What changes if the request comes from an even more senior person?”
- After Session 6: “A supplier might miss a delivery next month. Which log fits that situation today?”
- After Session 7: “If a milestone date is met but two agreed templates are missing, what can the date alone fail to show?”
- After Session 8: “If both defects are explained but not fixed, what extra evidence would you need?”
- After Session 10: “Which governance responsibilities remain even when development is iterative?”
- After Session 11: “Where should the team discuss how the missed check happened?”
- After Session 12: “What would change if a new estimate increased the remaining work?”

## Tutor answer guidance

This section contains spoilers. Use it to assess the reasoning, not to require exact wording.

**Session 1.** The work is to administer lending and process accurate, timely payments with reliable records and controls. “Build a new system” is an output, not the underlying business job. First hint: follow one payment from instruction to reconciliation. Then ask the learner to name one measurable improvement.

**Session 2.** Establish the problem, expected benefits, realistic options, costs, risks and investment justification. The Business Case compares options; authorising a project and buying a product are not the same decision. First hint: ask what management would need to know to choose between replacing, extending or tolerating the old system. Do not demand that the learner know document names before understanding their purpose.

**Session 3.** No. The Project Manager coordinates and is responsible for many activities, but authority depends on the activity and agreed governance. The Project Owner, Steering Committee and governance body have distinct powers. First hint: responsibility for preparing an investment proposal is different from authority to fund it. Transfer: Product Owner Marie prioritises the Work Items List with business input; Project Owner Florence retains the project's business perspective.

**Session 4.** The Business Implementation Plan addresses training and adoption. The Transition Plan addresses moving the solution into use, including the technical and operational switch. They must be coordinated; training is not an isolated classroom event. First hint: distinguish changing the system's operating environment from changing how the staff perform their jobs.

**Session 5.** Capture the request in the Change Request Form and Change Log, then assess impacts and send it through the agreed decision process. The requester being senior does not remove the need for evidence. The Project Manager should not silently approve, reject or start the work just because it sounds small. A verbal request becomes a traceable proposal before implementation.

**Session 6.** Update and close the materialised risk entry according to the case process, open issue I-14, cross-reference the records, assign ownership and assess the response. A separate approved change may be needed if the response alters the baseline. First hint: compare “might happen” with “has happened”. Avoid treating an issue as a risk with a likelihood of one hundred per cent while leaving management action unrecorded.

**Session 7.** Each euro spent has produced ninety cents of budgeted completed work. Cost efficiency is below the baseline comparison. It is not a ten per cent loss of cash, profit margin or a direct measure of days late. For deeper work, use the exact figures in the numbers section. Show the formula if missing prerequisites prevent the learner from deriving it.

**Session 8.** Examine the agreed criteria and tolerances, evidence from tests, the impact of both defects, who can accept, and any conditions or remedial plan. Marie's user validation is evidence; formal acceptance authority is a separate role. In this story, explained but unresolved breaks prompt recorded partial acceptance and further testing. First hint: a matching percentage omits what the two failures actually do.

**Session 9.** Hand over a named benefit owner, measures, measurement frequency, evidence source and reporting date, as well as unresolved operational actions and contract obligations. The project can close before longer-term benefits are fully measured. Do not claim benefits already achieved because the platform went live. First hint: distinguish the platform delivered, a changed business process, and an observed business improvement.

**Session 10.** Short iterations produce something users can inspect, allowing learning and reprioritisation while usable value accumulates. The reason is uncertainty and feedback, not the assertion that agile is always faster. Governance, project justification, authority, controls and closure remain. First hint: compare a report someone imagines with a report they actually try to use.

**Session 11.** No, not if the required check is part of the Definition of Done and is incomplete. Acceptance criteria describe the particular item; the shared standard also applies. Record the situation honestly and plan the remaining work. First hint: a report can show the requested numbers and still fail the team's quality standard. Review gathers product evidence and feedback; Retrospective investigates how to improve the team's work. A changed Definition of Done must follow the agreed process, not be weakened retroactively to make a score look better.

**Session 12.** Fifty-two points exceed the simple forecast capacity. Discuss priorities and feasible trade-offs, with the Product Owner, team and the project's governance as appropriate. Revisit assumptions and dependencies; do not simply demand a higher velocity or relabel unfinished work. The figure is a forecast, not a promise. First hint: ask what can change when scope does not fit the available period. Then ask the learner to translate the gap into business language for a senior stakeholder.

## Retrieval practice and progress handoff

At the start of the next session, ask one question from an earlier topic before teaching the new one. Mix pairs that are easily confused: risk and issue; baseline and forecast; Project Owner and Product Owner; acceptance criteria and Definition of Done; Review and Retrospective; output and benefit. Ask for an example, not only a definition.

Use three evidence labels: **needs explanation**, **can explain with a hint**, and **can explain and apply independently**. These describe the answers observed in this conversation, not a permanent ability or an exam prediction. Recheck an apparently learned concept in a later session.

Copyable handoff:

```text
PM² voice study handoff
Pack: CASTOR and POLLUX voice study pack
Date:
Language and preferred pace:
Sessions discussed:
Concepts applied independently, with evidence:
Concepts that still needed a hint:
Specific misunderstanding to revisit:

One next opening question:

```

The tutor fills this from the actual conversation. Do not invent completion, accuracy or confidence. Leave untested topics untested. A useful closing recap is two or three sentences plus this handoff, not a new examination.

## Source notes and case caveats

The pack draws on the repository's [CASTOR case bible](../CASE_BIBLE_CASTOR.md), [POLLUX case bible](../CASE_BIBLE_POLLUX.md), and the story files in `src/story/` and `src/pollux/`. Scene IDs repeat between projects: always identify both project and scene. The sources remain part of the repository; the attachment itself contains the information required for the sessions above.

Useful source anchors are CASTOR s01 for the operational problem; s15 for risk scoring; s21 for acceptance planning; s25 for business implementation; s33 for earned value; s36 for change control; s38 for the risk becoming an issue; s44 for partial acceptance; and s49 to s51 for benefit handover and closure. POLLUX s01 explains the reporting problem; s10 covers iteration planning; s14 covers priorities; s15 to s17 separate quality, review and retrospective; s18 covers forecasts; and s24 connects retrospectives with project-level lessons learned.

Several source shortcuts need care when studying:

- CASTOR's stated duration and dated lifecycle disagree, as noted above. Preserve the milestone dates rather than repairing the duration by inventing a different start date.
- CASTOR s25 plans benefit measurement from the first quarter of 2029. Later s49 calls for quarterly measurement from the third quarter of 2028 and a full-year 2029 statement in early 2030. Use the later scene when discussing closure, but recognise the changed schedule; the sources do not clearly document its formal revision.
- CASTOR s15 separates colour bands from escalation authority. An earlier project facts summary conflated the two. Use the distinction in the worked example above and do not convert ordinal likelihood scores into probabilities.
- An original POLLUX facts entry calls the Definition of Done “unchanging”, while s18 explicitly describes a revision to enforce checks. Use the scene's evidence that standards can be improved through an agreed process.
- Dialogue rounds CASTOR earned value to €2.9 million. Use the exact €2,898,000 for the calculations. The final reported cost is a later result, not the earlier forecast.
- The case's “common backstop” is a planned capability within its fictional timeline. Do not infer current treaty status from the story.

For formal methodology, consult the relevant edition of the [European Commission PM² materials](https://commission.europa.eu/about/departments-and-executive-agencies/digital-services/pm2-project-management-methodology_en). This pack paraphrases the local learning case; it does not independently validate every official responsibility row, page reference or examination claim. When exact methodology is disputed, compare the actual guide passage before treating the case as authoritative.
